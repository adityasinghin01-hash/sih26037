## 3 · BUFFALO — Murrah
Male withers **142 cm**, female **133 cm** · body length **150 / 148 cm** ·
heart girth 220 / 202 cm · weight 450–800 / 350–700 kg.
Jet black, **tightly curled horns** ("murrah" means curled), sparse hair, wet hide sheen.

## 4 · RHESUS MACAQUE — the bridge troop
Body **47–53 cm** · tail **21–23 cm** · height on all fours **41–57 cm** ·
males 7.7–11.7 kg, females 5.3–9.1 kg. Pale grey-brown, pink face, red-brown lower back.
**Troop: mean 35, range 20–200.** Composition ≈ **2 adult females per adult male**, ~1 immature
per adult, infants more than double the juveniles.
Roadside troops in northern India are documented and normal — 83 groups counted over 1651 km.
**Tails up and curved is the silhouette that identifies them at any distance.**

## 5 · HUMANS
Comfortable speed **1.2–1.4 m/s** · step **~0.70 m** (stride 1.44 m) · cadence 90–120,
average ~102/min. Stride × cadence must equal travel speed or they moonwalk.

## 7 · TREES
**Neem (*Azadirachta indica*) — the most planted roadside tree in India, and our hero.**
Mature **15–25 m** (occasionally 30–40) · crown **15–20 m** on old free-standing trees ·
trunk **30–90 cm** · **branches at 2–5 m** into a broad dense round or oval crown.
Young roadside specimens 8–14 m.
**Median species (not verge species): gulmohar, amaltas.** Narrow city streets: ashoka, silver oak.
Wide highways: rain tree. **Peepal belongs at a shrine or junction, not scattered on a verge.**
**Banyan is NOT the default Indian roadside tree** — temples, village centres, old trunk roads.
**Eucalyptus / safeda**: 20–50 m, up to 2 m diameter; roadside rows 20–25 m tall, 0.35 m trunks,
pale peeling bark, sparse high canopy, no low branches, planted 4–10 m apart in rows.

**CANOPY INTERLOCK — the rule that builds a green tunnel:**
crowns begin interacting at **10–15 m spacing**; **below 10 m they merge**, and a tree with
close neighbours grows **elongated along the row and narrower across it**. Over 15 m they read
as separate trees.
Crown *cover* can be 100 % while crown *closure* is under 100 % — overlapping parasol crowns
give complete cover from above while light still enters at oblique angles. **That is the
dappled light on the road.**
Build target: **8–10 m spacing both sides, ~62 % measured cover, with real holes in it.**

## 8 · GROUND VEGETATION — seven layers, and the species are known
1. **Kans grass (*Saccharum spontaneum*) — up to 3 m**, feathery white plumes. This is the tall
   pale mass in drive_02 / drive_09 / drive_13. **It is not scrub and not always sugarcane.**
2. **Sugarcane** — millable cane **225 cm** at harvest; rows **60–150 cm** (western UP advised
   120–150 cm).
3. Shrub layer 0.8–1.4 m — lantana, calotropis, in patches.
4. Mid grasses 0.30–0.60 m — *Bothriochloa bladhii*, *Pennisetum pedicellatum* (roadside).
5. **Doob (*Cynodon dactylon*) 0.05–0.15 m** — dominates disturbed and moist ground, grazed short.
6. Weeds and seedlings filling every gap.
7. Floor: leaves, twigs, dung, litter, dust.
*Desmostachya bipinnata* (kush) also common on north Indian roadsides.

## 9 · THE FARMING CALENDAR — late September, western UP
Kharif is sown June–July and harvested **September–October**. In late September:
**paddy harvesting is under way**, and **sugarcane harvest runs August to November**, so cane
is standing and being cut. The monsoon **retreats from north-west India around 17 September**
and is gone by 15 October — so the ground is drying, not wet.
**Field activity to show:** cane standing at full height, paddy being cut and stacked, ploughed
plots being prepared for rabi, farmers with sickles, bundles carried on heads, a tractor with a
trolley, cattle grazing stubble, egrets following them.
**And the brick kiln is COLD** — Bull's Trench kilns fire January to June only. No smoke plume
in September. Chimney **minimum 27 m in UP**; yard **20–30 m × 75–120 m**; 20,000–50,000
bricks/day.

## 10 · SOIL
**Bhabar (hill foot):** alluvial, shallow, **top layers full of small pebbles and stones.**
**Terai / plain:** rich in nitrogen and organic matter, sand and clay.
Two-tone rule for a forest floor: **dark, near-black humus in hollows, gullies and under closed
canopy** (litter collects and stays damp) · **light pebbly tan on ridges and sunlit slopes**
(litter washes off and dries). The boundary is a noise-masked gradient, never a line.

## 11 · BIRDS
House crow, common myna, rock pigeon, cattle egret, black kite, red-vented bulbul, pied
kingfisher. **Different tolerance is the point:** crows and mynas flush at ~8 m, pigeons flush
all at once, kites and egrets ignore a car completely. That is the same idea as the planner's
per-agent negotiability, and it should be visible.

## 13 · HILL STREAMS AND WATERFALLS  (added 3 Sep)
Seasonal Shivalik streams are locally called **choes, khads or raus**. They swell in the
monsoon and carry **heavy sediment**, which is why their beds are boulders, cobbles and sand
rather than mud.
**Measured catchment figures for the Uttarakhand Shivalik: stream frequency 9.36 channels per
km², drainage density 4.55 km of channel per km², relief 220 m.**
**That is the number that justifies the gullies.** A 4.55 km/km² drainage density means a hill
face is cut by many small channels, not one or two — so the hill must carry a branching network
of shallow gullies, not a smooth flank with a token crease.
Waterfalls in this belt are **seasonal cascades over a rock step**, alive in and just after the
monsoon (late September still counts), reduced to a stain on the rock by winter.
