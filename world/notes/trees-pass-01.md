## The one thing that repeats in 4 of 6 images
**The canopy closes over the road.** Branches reach out horizontally at 4-6 m and meet
overhead. The road is a green tunnel, not an open strip with trees beside it.

Two consequences:
1. It is the single most recognisable feature. Get it and the scene reads Indian instantly.
2. **It solves the horizon problem.** A closed canopy hides the sky-image background that
   ruined shot01 — no European car park visible if there is a roof of leaves.

## What makes an Indian roadside tree, structurally
- **Aerial roots.** Long thin strands hanging in curtains from horizontal limbs, some reaching
  the ground and thickening into secondary trunks. **No stock library tree has these.** This
  is the feature that no amount of scaling a jacaranda will fake.
- **Fused, buttressed base.** Not one clean trunk - a mass of merged stems flaring outward at
  ground level, often 2-3 m across.
- **Low split.** Major limbs leave the trunk at 2-3 m, not high up.
- **Horizontal spread beats height.** Canopy much wider than tall.
- **Pale grey-white smooth bark** on the ficus; dark gnarled bark on the tamarind/neem type.
- **Leaves:** ficus = heart-shaped, glossy, medium, bright green. The other type = fine
  pinnate compound leaves, dark, feathery.

## Ground and light
- **Red laterite earth** at the road edge in both banyan images. Not brown soil - red.
- Road edge crumbles straight into that earth. White edge line, no kerb.
- **Dappled shadow** on the tarmac through the canopy. Hard-edged, high contrast, moving.
  This is half the look of image 06 and costs nothing to reproduce.

## Verdict for the build
Poly Haven's four broadleaf models cannot produce any of this. **Build a ficus:** fused
buttressed base, low horizontal limbs, and aerial-root curtains generated procedurally.
The roots are the differentiator and they are geometry we can generate, not something to
download.
